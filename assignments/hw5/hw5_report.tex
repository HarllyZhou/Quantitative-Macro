    \item The empirical responses contradicts with the Bornbusch model and the Eichenbaum-Evans paper. The EXR index at time $t$, $I_t$, is given by
    \begin{align*}
        I_t = I_{t-1} \prod_{j=1}^{J_t} \left(\frac{e_{j,t}}{e_{j,t-1}}\right)^{\omega_{j,t}},
    \end{align*}
    where $J_t$ is the number of foreign currencies, $e_{j,t}$ is the price of US dollars \textbf{in terms of the foreign currency $j$}, and $\omega_{j,t}$ is the weight of the foreign currency $j$ in the basket.\footnote{See \url{https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2000/200006/200006pap.pdf} for this definition.} So when the exchange rate increases, the US dollar appreciates. The overshooting result argues that when FFR increases, the US dollar should appreciate and keep appreciating for a while. However, the empirical responses in question 3 and 4 shows that the US dollar depreciates for a year and then starts to appreciate back.

    Two possible reasons are as follows
    \begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*.]
        \item In their paper, commodity prices are included, while we do not. Commodity prices are forward-looking variables, which may capture some of the Fed's information when they consider the FFR as an instrument.
        \item The paper was in the 1990s. In particular, the Eichenbaum-Evans papers used the data from 1974 to 1990. Therefore, I also plotted the IRF for this period. And We do observe an appreciation after 1 quarter. However, there is still a considerable size of depreciation after around 1 year.

    \end{enumerate}
